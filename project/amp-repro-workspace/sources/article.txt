New antibiotics are urgently needed to help with rising levels of antimicrobial
resistance, but searching for new therapeutics is often a slow, labor-intensive
process. With Google Antigravity, our agentic development platform, this process
can now be made easier.
Google Antigravity provides an agentic science workbench that empowers
biologists to discover, structurally evaluate, and prioritize promising targets quickly,
compressing what used to be a fragmented search over multiple databases into a
single session. It also helps researchers scan through millions of previously
uncharacterized proteins in the UniProt database using ProtNLM, a model that
predicts biological function from amino acid sequences.
This technical walkthrough shows how biologists used Google Antigravity to uncover
a novel antimicrobial peptide candidate which has predicted activity against the
pathogens involved in respiratory tract infections. The lead identified here is now
undergoing wet-lab validation.

Finding new antimicrobial peptides
Rising antimicrobial resistance is one of the most pressing global health crises of our
time and could result in an estimated 10 million annual deaths globally by 2050. To
prevent this, we urgently need to discover novel therapeutics. Antimicrobial
peptides (AMPs), particularly bacteriocins, offer a promising alternative to
traditional antibiotics because they have naturally evolved to target fundamental
vulnerabilities in competing bacteria, from cell envelopes to essential intracellular
machinery, making it difficult for pathogens to develop resistance.
The exponential growth of genomic and metagenomic sequencing has populated
global databases with billions of natural amino acid sequences. This pool of natural
data has already produced globally used bacteriocins like Nisin, alongside recent
genome-mined breakthroughs such as Darobactin, Dynobactin, and Lariocidin.
Many, however, remain locked within the unannotated "dark matter" of protein
databases, with their function uncharacterized because they aren’t similar to known
proteins. For example, the world's leading public resource of protein sequence and
functional information, UniProt, holds millions of sequences with unknown
functionality, transferred directly from genome sequencing projects.
To bridge this gap, Google developed ProtNLM (Protein Natural Language Model), a
deep learning model trained to directly read the underlying evolutionary “grammar”

Collaborative intelligence
Scientific discovery is rarely a single step process; finding viable candidate proteins
requires human intuition and iteration to incorporate new empirical evidence.
Working in Antigravity, the researcher and AI agent begin by setting a focused
biological goal: find and computationally validate novel AMPs in Streptococcus
pseudopneumoniae. During an interactive, multi-turn session the researcher steers
the scientific strategy, deciding which questions to ask and what evidence to collect,
while the agent handles the heavy lifting of cross-database orchestration, structural
analysis, and literature synthesis.
Overview of the ProtNLM pipeline translating raw amino acid sequences into natural-language
biological annotations for unreviewed UniProtKB/TrEMBL entries. The functional annotation scope
of the system covers protein names, function comments, subcellular locations, and GO terms, in
addition to keywords.

A shared 3D canvas for interactive collaboration
A protein's 3D structure can help us understand its biological function. Examining a
candidate’s predicted fold in space is key. To provide this end-to-end workflow in a
single workspace, we are introducing the Biomolecular Structure Viewer, a 3D
visualization tool embedded directly into Antigravity.
The researcher and agent work together over a shared visual state. As the agent
retrieves structures from AlphaFold Database or the scientist’s own predictions, it
can dynamically drive the viewer. For instance, it can highlight key functional regions
to visually explain its hypotheses. Crucially, this awareness flows both ways: actions
like highlighting specific residues or pinning notes directly onto a binding pocket are
synchronised back to the agent. A researcher can simply mark a region of interest in
3D space and ask, for example, "How might this β -strand affect Lipid II binding?",
allowing the agent to immediately reason over the exact structural context the
scientist is looking at.
The Biomolecular Structure Viewer inside Antigravity showing the AlphaFold-predicted structure

annotate functional regions.

Accelerating Scientific Discovery
Historically, uncovering insights like those in the workflow demonstrated above
required fragmented and specialised software: writing custom scripts to query
databases, manually exporting files to 3D modelling programs, managing
disconnected genomic tools and working with multiple experts to design the wet lab
assays. Because wet-lab validation remains resource-intensive, narrowing the search
space to only the highest-confidence leads upfront is critical. Using Google
Antigravity allows researchers to navigate the vast biological search space efficiently
while leveraging accurate protein function predictions from a domain-specific
model.
Google Antigravity executes this end-to-end scientific reasoning in one place. This
allows scientists to spend more time evaluating high-quality hypotheses, running
experiments in the lab and solving research challenges.
